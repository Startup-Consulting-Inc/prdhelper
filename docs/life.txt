The Engineer Who Got Hired to Wipe Bodies: A Field Report on Identity Collapse
The first time I had to say "I am a caregiver" out loud, the sentence stuck in my throat like a bone. We were at a party. My wife's colleagues, people who didn't know me. Someone asked the question that everyone asks: So what do you do?
I opened my mouth to say something about AI. About the consulting work. About the startup I was planning. Instead I said, "I work in home care." The silence was brief but absolute. Then: "Oh, that's... rewarding."
What I heard was demotion. Failure. What happened to him? I saw it in their faces. I saw it in my own face later, in the bathroom mirror at the immobile man's house, washing my hands between feedings. The man looking back at me was fifty-something, with the softening jaw of someone who has stopped fighting time, wearing cargo pants with stains he didn't want to identify.
I'd built data platforms at a Fortune 500 fintech for nearly twenty years. Now I was lifting bodies for almost minimum wage. This is a field report from inside that gap.
The Job You Apply For When the Doors Stop Opening
After the layoff, the first months felt like a sabbatical. Severance, unemployment, ski trips, postponed projects. I built a platform that scored job postings against my resume, tailored applications, tracked submissions. It was a beautiful piece of engineering. It was exactly what I would have built at my old job.
It collected rejections perfectly.
The market was flooded with engineers who had my exact skill set. Month after month — silence, automated rejections, the polite "we've decided to move forward with another candidate." So I tried lower. Factory work. Warehouse jobs. They wouldn't hire me either. Overqualified. They saw twenty years of engineering and assumed I'd leave the moment something better came along. They weren't wrong.
That's the trap nobody warns you about. Too senior for the senior roles, because the market already has hundreds of you. Too senior for the junior roles, because you're a flight risk. The doors don't slam. They just don't open.
Then a home care agency called. The pay was almost minimum wage. The work was lifting, cleaning, feeding, sitting with people who couldn't thank me. I said yes. I was happy — actually happy — that someone wanted me. I could pay bills. I was no longer useless.
I just couldn't say what I'd become.
What Frankl Knew About the Vacuum
Viktor Frankl, who survived the camps, wrote about what he called the existential vacuum — the inner emptiness that arrives when meaning derived from work and status is removed. "The unemployed worker," he wrote, "is harassed by a sense of emptiness and the resulting lack of meaning in his life."
That's the mechanism. When the scaffolding of your self gets pulled out, you don't just adjust. You enter a vacuum. And the vacuum is where despair lives — the slow-onset kind that shows up at 3 AM when the mortgage is due, when LinkedIn shows you a former colleague's promotion, when the question who am I now arrives before coffee does and you can't answer it.
Frankl's response wasn't to find a new identity that resembles the old one. He pointed to three pathways: creative work, loving encounter, and the stance you take toward unavoidable suffering.
I didn't find this comforting at first. I found it inadequate. I wanted my old career back, not philosophy. But the philosophy is what's been keeping me upright while the career stays gone.
The Body Becomes the Tool
A man who built systems that scale now holds a body that cannot move. A man who taught optimization now bathes a man who cannot remember his wife's name. The dissonance isn't intellectual. It lives in the shoulders when I lift the immobile man from his chair. It lives in the knees when I kneel to tie an 88-year-old's shoes. My body has become my primary tool. And unlike my code, it doesn't improve with iteration. It only gets older.
But something else has been happening. Slowly.
I have a regular client who can communicate only by opening or closing his mouth. Open means yes. Closed means no. That's the entire protocol. One bit of information, no error correction, no acknowledgment packet. If I were still at my old desk, I'd call this a broken API and file a ticket. Instead I sit. I wait. I read the angle of his jaw and try to honor it as a signal rather than a null value.
That waiting is the work. Not the lifting. Not the cleaning. The waiting — the willingness to sit in not-knowing without rushing to a conclusion that makes my life easier.
It turns out that's a skill. It's not on any resume. No recruiter screens for it. But it's the one I most lacked when I was an engineer, and it's the one I'm finally learning.
What Hiring Filters Don't Capture
I spent my career building the kind of systems that filtered candidates, scored resumes, optimized hiring pipelines. I know exactly how I became the outlier they discarded. The same logic I once wrote into code is now what's keeping me from being hired.

The man who's been "out of the market" for eighteen months while caregiving has been doing emotional and ethical work that is structurally inefficient — and that's the point. Care doesn't scale. It doesn't parallelize. It can't be load-balanced across a population. Every encounter is unique. Every Tuesday afternoon is a new ethical event. The person doing it is learning, in their hands and shoulders, the limits of the optimization mindset that AI is now forcing the entire economy to confront.
If you're building AI products, that's not a gap on a resume. That's exactly the perspective your team is missing.
What I'd Tell Someone in the Same Place
If you're reading this from inside the same gap — laid off, applying everywhere, watching your savings shrink while your identity quietly comes apart — I can't tell you it gets fixed. Mine isn't fixed. The mortgage is still there. The career I had is still gone.
But three things have helped me keep moving, and they might help you.
First, separate your worth from the market's verdict on you. The market is wrong about a lot of people right now, especially senior ones. Its rejection of you is information about its current state, not about your value. Hold that line, even when it sounds like cope. Especially when it sounds like cope.
Second, take the work that's available, even if it's beneath your training. Not because dignity lives in suffering, but because being in motion is better than being in stasis. The caregiver job didn't restore my old identity. It gave me a new place to stand while I figure out what comes next. That turned out to matter more than the wage.
Third, keep building. After every shift, I open my laptop and work on something — writing, AI tools, teaching material. Not because the side projects will save me. Because they remind me I'm still a builder. Frankl called this creative value, one of his three pathways to meaning. It's a rope. It works.
The mouth opens. The mouth closes. That's the whole sentence I now live inside. Everything else is what we bring to the silence.
